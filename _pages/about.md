I study learning in humans and machines, in individuals and in groups, with the aim of helping people learn and act better together.

I work at the intersection of machine learning and cognitive science. I use neural networks to build computational theories of human learning, use AI as a tool for scientific discovery (for example, behavioural cloning), and study AI systems, including the values they learn, with the methods informed by cognitive science. I also work on philosophy of science and metascience: science itself is a form of collective learning, increasingly one in which humans learn alongside AI. In my new lab, these strands come together where the psychological meets the social—how individual minds shape, and are shaped by, the groups they learn and act in.

I am currently a postdoctoral researcher in [Chris Summerfield's group](https://humaninformationprocessing.com/) at the University of Oxford. In December 2026 I will join the [School of Psychology and Neuroscience](https://www.gla.ac.uk/schools/psychologyneuroscience/) at the University of Glasgow as a Lecturer. I did my PhD at the University of Montreal, at the International Laboratory for Brain, Music & Sound Research (BRAMS) and Mila (the Quebec AI Institute), where I compared representations in artificial neural networks and the human auditory system.

#### Where I'm heading

Cognitive science has traditionally studied the abilities of individuals in isolation. But in real life, we rarely do anything truly alone: we are always learning and working together. Humanity's greatest achievements come from our capacity to learn from and with each other and to organize ourselves around shared goals, and many of the defining crises of our time reflect our failure to do so. 
<!-- There is also mounting evidence that what is uniquely human about our learning and reasoning is inextricably linked to our sociality. -->

At Glasgow, I will continue to study the computational principles of human learning while centring the fact that all human learning is socially embedded. When people learn together, a group can become wiser than any of its members, or it can amplify its members' errors and biases. I want to know what separates the wisdom of crowds from the madness of mobs. When does learning with others, by discussing, explaining, and teaching, help us learn more than learning alone? Which patterns of communication help a community learn, and which lead it astray? And how might tools that help each individual, such as generative AI, harm the community as a whole, for example by homogenizing the group? You can read more about these questions on my [research page]({{ '/research/' | relative_url }}).

These questions matter well beyond the lab. Many of the problems we now face, such as climate change, can only be solved by large groups acting together, and that is exactly where coordination is hardest. As Nick Chater and George Loewenstein write in _It's On You_, paraphrasing Mancur Olson, "the concentrated interests of the few are so powerful because they can most easily be aligned and coordinated to carry through a coherent plan of action. But coordinated action by the many, once achieved, is unstoppable." In a hyperconnected, globalized world, our actions have far-reaching effects that are difficult to anticipate. We did not evolve to reason in such a world. I want to do research that supports the collective intelligence and coordinated action of the many, perhaps with the help of new technologies (or at least while minimizing their harms).
